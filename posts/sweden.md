There are places where, after a few hours, you start feeling like you've been on the road for far too long. And then there are places like this: endless open landscapes, rugged mountains, small lakes, vast wetlands and seemingly infinite space.

In the summer of 2025, I spent three days exploring the Blåfjella–Skjækerfjella region and the surrounding mountain landscapes. My route initially took me through Grønningen on the Norwegian side before continuing towards Sweden and into Jämtland.

As always, my trusty companion was my Seat Alhambra.

 By that point, it had already clocked up nearly 400,000 kilometres. Not exactly the vehicle most people would choose for serious overlanding. But it had taken me to plenty of places where others probably wouldn't even consider driving.

And that was exactly the point: three days of off-road driving, sleeping outdoors and spending as much time as possible in a landscape that felt almost entirely my own.


**A Scandinavian Summer That Didn't Live Up to Its Reputation**

The summer of 2025 was exceptionally warm across Scandinavia. Temperatures exceeding 30°C were not uncommon – quite remarkable for this region and time of year.



The landscape itself, however, is anything but dry. These mountain and wetland areas are characterised by damp ground, countless small bodies of water and extensive bogs.

Beautiful as all of that is, it comes with a rather unpleasant side effect in summer: mosquitoes. And not just the occasional little nuisance buzzing around your head in the evening. There were an incredible number of them.
<figure class="story-inline story-inline-right"><img src="https://86ec824d1amijkwd.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/blog-inline/1794498261951-IMG_1939-8vM0IJ5q1TOnXyRdIkvbi5g0YiVP11.jpeg" alt="Keep yourself SAFE from Mosquitoes" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Keep yourself SAFE from Mosquitoes</figcaption></figure>


On top of that, the midday heat brought out the horseflies. Anyone who has ever tried to enjoy an open landscape in temperatures above 30°C while being hunted by horseflies probably knows exactly what I'm talking about.

Mornings in the rooftop tent were particularly entertaining. Sometimes you didn't even need to look outside to know what was waiting for you. If you stayed in bed a little too long, eventually you could hear them. That deep, ominous buzzing right outside the tent.

That's when you knew it was time to get up. Quickly. Because the moment you opened the zipper, the daily battle against the flying insect population began.

**Three Days of Freedom**

Apart from the little and not-so-little pests, the area was exactly what I had been looking for.

I spent a lot of time hiking, exploring the surroundings and, of course, fishing. The lakes and rivers were beautiful, and I actually managed to catch some really lovely trout.

These are the moments that make trips like this so special to me: sitting somewhere by the water, taking in the surroundings, perhaps catching a fish, and otherwise having absolutely nothing to do.
<figure class="story-inline story-inline-full"><img src="https://86ec824d1amijkwd.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/blog-inline/1794498373642-IMG_1772-v9YUM4BpUIoqYpHEN4ALWyNlPKdFTU.jpeg" alt="Reisefoto" loading="lazy" /></figure>

No appointments, no crowds, no noise. Just the landscape and the time to really experience it.

From a biological perspective, the area also had plenty to offer. One of my personal highlights was finding several representatives of the genus Drosera – the sundews, a group of fascinating carnivorous plants.


<figure class="story-inline story-inline-full"><img src="https://86ec824d1amijkwd.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/blog-inline/1794498463571-IMG_1773-6jcVFmzfl6HyoRmdaUPy1cbQNd0T7a.jpeg" alt="Drosera rotundifolia in Norwegian/Swedish Highlands" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Drosera rotundifolia in Norwegian/Swedish Highlands</figcaption></figure>


Naturally, that made my biologist's heart beat a little faster. These inconspicuous plants thrive in nutrient-poor, wetland habitats and use their sticky tentacles to catch small insects, supplementing the nutrients they cannot obtain from the soil.

Sometimes you don't need a spectacular wildlife encounter. A few square metres of bog can be more than enough reason for a biologist to stop and take a closer look.

**An Encounter I Won't Forget**

One encounter, however, was considerably larger than a sundew.

During my time there, I actually managed to spot a small family of bears.

Brown bears are still present in these border regions, particularly on the Swedish side of this vast landscape. Still, knowing that bears live in an area is one thing. Suddenly finding yourself looking at them is quite another.

The encounter was fascinating, but it also made me feel a little uneasy. After all, I was sleeping outside – in my rooftop tent, on top of my car.

And while a rooftop tent at least gives you the feeling of being slightly removed from the ground, an encounter with bears quickly reminds you that you are not exactly in a controlled environment.

It certainly made me realise just how much wilderness still exists in these regions. You don't have to travel to the most remote corners of Alaska to be reminded that you're not alone out there.

**Solitude as a Luxury**

What I will probably remember most about those three days isn't actually the bear family, the trout or the carnivorous plants.

It's the silence.

In this area, you can genuinely spend days travelling without encountering another soul. No hiking groups, no crowded car parks, no tourist hotspots. You drive, go for a hike, find somewhere to sleep and simply enjoy being outdoors.

For anyone who values peace and enjoys exploring independently, that is an incredible luxury.

Of course, you need to bring a few things along. A vehicle you can rely on – or at least one you know well enough to understand what you can reasonably put it through. Because when you're travelling far away from the usual routes, a breakdown becomes a very different situation from having one in a supermarket car park.

My Alhambra once again proved its worth. Nearly 400,000 kilometres on the clock, a rooftop tent on top and plenty of gravel, dust and unpaved roads behind it. Maybe it's not the typical overlanding vehicle. But it has character. And apparently, it still has plenty of adventures left in it.

**Three Days to Remember**

Three days isn't a particularly long time. But sometimes three days are more than enough to clear your head.

I saw a lot, caught some beautiful fish, discovered fascinating plants, spotted a family of bears and dealt with an impressive number of Scandinavian mosquitoes and horseflies.

It was hot, occasionally exhausting and not always particularly comfortable. But that's part of the experience.

What remains is the memory of a landscape where you can still truly lose yourself. Evenings spent outdoors, quiet waters and the feeling of being a long way away from everyday life.

And perhaps that's what overlanding is really about for me: not driving as far as possible or trying to experience as much as possible, but finding a place where you simply want to stay.

Even if getting out of your tent in the morning means facing an entire squadron of horseflies.
