**A Promising Start**

When I bought the T4, I knew I wasn't getting a brand-new vehicle. It was a 30-year-old former utility vehicle that had already seen quite a bit of life. It had been used for work, taken on the Carbage Run through Bosnia and Serbia and had clearly accumulated its fair share of scratches, dents and stories along the way.

Still, I was reasonably optimistic.

The truck came with a valid German roadworthiness inspection (TÜV), with more than a year remaining. It was registered, running and driving. From the outside, it certainly wasn't a beauty queen, but I had seen T4s in considerably worse visual condition.
I knew there would be some maintenance and repairs involved. That's simply part of buying an older vehicle. What I hadn't quite anticipated was the actual extent of the problems.
Before thinking about expedition equipment, off-road modifications or travelling around Africa, I first needed to find out what kind of mechanical and structural condition the vehicle was really in.

So, it was time for a proper inspection.

**The Engine – Surprisingly Healthy**

Let's start with the good news.
The heart of the T4 is the familiar 2.4-litre naturally aspirated diesel, the AAB. Five cylinders, 78 horsepower and a fairly straightforward mechanical design.
And surprisingly, the engine itself made a pretty good impression.
It sounded healthy and ran reasonably well. There was a noticeable ticking or knocking noise, which I suspected was coming from the vacuum pump. A fairly typical issue on these engines and nothing particularly unusual for an older T4.
There were, however, a few things that needed attention.
The injection pump was leaking at two different points, and both the timing belt and auxiliary belt were due for replacement. The water pump was also on the list.
Nothing particularly dramatic, but definitely things that needed to be addressed before heading out on a long journey.

Considering the age of the vehicle, I was actually quite happy with the engine. At least one major component seemed to be in relatively good shape.
The rest of the vehicle, however, had a different opinion.

**The Bodywork – Where Things Started to Get Interesting**

The real surprises were waiting underneath.

The rear right spring mounting point was another major problem. There was a rather impressive hole where there really shouldn't have been one.

The front right jacking point wasn't much better. When I tried to lift the vehicle, the mounting point simply collapsed under the pressure.
That's always a reassuring moment when working on a car.

The frame underneath the flatbed was also heavily corroded. And speaking of the flatbed: the plywood floor had seen better days, too.
More than half of the original plywood platform was rotten, soft and literally crumbling away. The rear sideboards weren't in particularly good condition either.
<figure class="story-inline story-inline-right"><img src="https://86ec824d1amijkwd.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/blog-inline/1795104019192-739F5B64-480B-4E3C-8400-78F6148FA680-TUiiKRgHwUUqN1Ek5XBs7xkVu83r12.jpeg" alt="Reisefoto" loading="lazy" /></figure>
At this point, it was becoming increasingly clear that the bodywork would require considerably more attention than I had originally planned.

**A Few More Surprises**

As I started looking closer and gradually working my way through the vehicle, more problems appeared.
One of the more serious discoveries was the cab mounting.
The driver's cab is welded to the vehicle's main frame at several points. Unfortunately, three of these mounting areas were severely corroded.
In other words, the connection between the cab and the frame was no longer intact in the way it should be.
This was certainly one of those moments when you stop for a second, look at the vehicle and wonder what exactly you have got yourself into.
<figure class="story-inline story-inline-left"><img src="https://86ec824d1amijkwd.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/blog-inline/1795104455992-IMG_4234-9XX3nM8H8gjRzLD8xj8Jfnvep2y5k4.jpeg" alt="Reisefoto" loading="lazy" /></figure>

There were also a few more conventional mechanical issues:
- A worn drop link.
- Tired rear shock absorbers.
- Worn steering tie rod ends.
- Brakes that were due for replacement.
<figure class="story-inline story-inline-right"><img src="https://86ec824d1amijkwd.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/blog-inline/1795104096718-IMG_4236-syd5usqtTNH7pMJVkPHNIG3SDypZJg.jpeg" alt="Reisefoto" loading="lazy" /></figure>

The brakes, however, were never really a concern. On a vehicle of this age, I consider things like that part of normal maintenance rather than unexpected major repairs. <figure class="story-inline story-inline-right"><img src="https://86ec824d1amijkwd.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/blog-inline/1795104133357-IMG_4627-0jvnSlWS4UFFcwKYWHcLUgiwMXa7GN.jpeg" alt="Reisefoto" loading="lazy" /></figure>

The same goes for many suspension components and rubber bushes. If you're planning to travel long distances, especially away from well-equipped workshops, you want to know exactly what condition these parts are in.

**And Then There Was the Paintwork**

Visually, the T4 wasn't exactly winning any beauty contests either.
The paintwork was in pretty poor condition, with stickers covering large parts of the vehicle, along with dents, scratches and plenty of signs of its previous life.
But honestly, that didn't bother me too much.
I wasn't looking for a perfectly restored classic vehicle or something that would win prizes at a VW meet.
I wanted a practical base for a project. Something with character that I could work on myself and gradually turn into a vehicle suitable for travelling.
And besides, a few scratches and dents are probably going to happen anyway once you start taking an expedition vehicle off the beaten track.

**A Bigger Project Than Expected**

Looking back, the most important realisation was that almost everything I had discovered so far was simply about getting the vehicle back into a solid and reliable condition.
We hadn't even started talking about the actual conversion.
No Syncro drivetrain, no suspension lift, no off-road equipment, no solar system and no camper conversion.
Just making sure that the vehicle itself was structurally sound and mechanically reliable enough to serve as a proper starting point.

I had bought a T4 with a valid roadworthiness inspection and a seemingly healthy engine. What I had actually bought was a vehicle that needed extensive structural repairs, plenty of mechanical attention and a fair amount of work before it could even begin to fulfil its intended purpose.

Still, I wasn't particularly discouraged.
I had bought the vehicle relatively cheaply, and I knew from the beginning that there would be work involved. I just hadn't expected quite this much of it.
But that's also part of the appeal of a project like this. You start with an idea, take things apart, discover problems, learn how things work and gradually figure out how to put everything back together again – hopefully a little better than before.

At this stage, I was still very much at the beginning of that learning process.

**First Things First**

Before thinking about how to turn the T4 into an expedition vehicle, I needed to make some fundamental decisions.
What actually needs to be repaired? Which components should be replaced while everything is apart? And perhaps most importantly: what kind of vehicle do I really need for the journeys I have in mind?
The original plan involved travelling independently, exploring remote places and eventually making my way around Africa.
A front-wheel-drive pickup with a rather questionable structural condition might not have been the ideal starting point for that ambition.
But I had a vehicle, a workshop full of things to learn and a rather stubborn idea in my head.
The next step would be to start bringing the old Double Cab back to life – and to think a little more seriously about what it would take to turn it into the expedition vehicle I had envisioned.
Because getting it roadworthy again was only the beginning.

**The real transformation was still ahead.**
